Teaching practice · Foundational and advanced courses

Enhancing Student Learning in the Age of AI: Teaching With and Without AI

A teaching strategy that supports learning with permitted AI assistance and through independent practice, shared discussion, and application.

Dimpy Gill · Computer Science Faculty · Clark College

My teaching approach

I build opportunities to observe learning throughout the class, rather than relying only on a finished submission. Students prepare, discuss concepts, and practice. I adapt the next steps to the course level: foundational courses use practice exercises and in-class quizzes, while advanced courses also use projects, explanations, and live scenarios. This approach helps me identify where students need support and gather evidence of their understanding, including when AI may have contributed to a project.

Set clear boundaries for AI use

In some courses, especially an AI course, using AI tools may be part of the learning objective. I allow AI where it supports the activity and specify which parts students must complete independently. Permission to use AI in one part of a project does not mean it is allowed in every part.

I distinguish between using the AI tools or models required by the course and using an assistant to generate answers or code. For each activity, I explain what assistance is permitted and what students need to demonstrate themselves.

Foundational courses: practice at home, check learning in class

In introductory courses such as CSE 120, students are still developing the basic concepts, and class activities do not yet center on projects. I assign take-home practice exercises and assignments and tell students to complete this work without AI assistance.

I do not rely solely on the assumption that a take-home submission was completed independently. Alongside homework, I use periodic in-class pop quizzes that students complete without AI. Students know in advance that a quiz may take place during any class session, so they are expected to keep up with the material and practice regularly.

These short quizzes revisit concepts students have learned and practiced. They let me check understanding over time and identify concepts that need further explanation or practice. A quiz provides evidence of what a student can do independently at that point; it does not establish whether AI was used on earlier homework.

Two complementary activities: take-home assignments provide ongoing practice; in-class pop quizzes provide recurring checks of independent learning. These quizzes serve a different purpose from the repeat-attempt formative quizzes used in my project-based sequence.

Advanced and AI courses: from preparation to application

01 · PREPARE

Share materials before class

I make course materials available in advance so students have an opportunity to preview the topic and return to the resources during practice.

02 · EXPLAIN

Develop understanding together

In class, I explain the topic and clarify the concepts students will use in the activities that follow.

03 · PRACTICE

Use formative quizzes

Students complete formative quizzes during class to revisit material they have read and heard. I allow two or three attempts so they can work toward full marks and reinforce their understanding. I observe their engagement as they work.

The purpose is practice and improvement; a perfect score after repeated attempts alone does not establish independent understanding.

04 · APPLY

Begin a topic-based project

After the formative quiz, I assign a small project or an end-to-end project based on the current topic and the concepts practiced in the quiz. Students begin during class, following the stated AI-use boundaries. If they cannot finish during the session, I ask them to submit their work by the end of the day.

05 · EXPLAIN AND REFLECT

Explain the process and explore a live scenario

Students explain what they built, how the process works, and why they made their choices. When a project has been completed at home, this conversation is especially useful for understanding the student's own learning.

I introduce a related scenario and invite the student to explain or demonstrate how to handle it live. For example: “Let’s explore what happens if the input changes. How could we adapt this together?” The discussion is supportive and conversational, framed as an opportunity for everyone to learn from one another rather than a high-pressure interrogation.

I use students' explanations, responses, and demonstrations alongside observations from class to understand what they can apply independently.

Individual support during class

In person, students work at individual computers while I move around the room and check their progress. Online, I use breakout rooms and ask students to share their screens when I visit so they can show their work.

I visit students at different points during the session and ask:

“What have you completed so far?”“What questions do you have?”“Is anything causing difficulty?”

These conversations allow me to see work in progress and provide guidance when it is needed. Breakout rooms also create a more private opportunity for students who feel hesitant to ask questions in front of the whole class. They can share concerns and receive individual support.

Supportive evidence of independent learning

Observe understanding without creating pressure

During activities designated for independent work, I ask students to complete the specified parts without AI assistance and observe their progress through classroom visits or screen sharing. Being in class or sharing a screen does not, by itself, establish that no AI was used.

A student's explanation and live application provide evidence of current understanding; they do not establish exactly how an earlier submission was produced. My focus is on what the student can explain and do, with guidance and clear expectations about permitted assistance.

I keep questions connected to the topic and use a collegial tone: “How might we approach this variation?” or “Could you walk us through the next step?” This makes the exchange part of shared learning while helping me identify where further support is needed.

My response to the AI assessment question

“My focus is on making students' learning visible throughout the process. I share materials before class and explain the concepts. In foundational courses such as CSE 120, I pair take-home practice assigned without AI with periodic in-class pop quizzes completed independently, so I can check learning over time. In advanced and AI courses, I use formative quizzes and have students begin topic-based projects during class. Through individual check-ins, screen sharing in online breakout rooms, and subsequent project explanations, I explore what students understand and where they need guidance. I set clear boundaries for AI use, including activities where AI tools are part of the learning objective and parts students complete independently. In project-based courses, a supportive live scenario invites students to explain and apply their learning so the whole class can learn together. My focus is on the student's understanding, whether the original project involved permitted AI assistance or independent work.”
