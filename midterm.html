Choose one project. The examples are possibilities, not a checklist of required topics. You are not required to learn agentic AI, classification, clustering, or any other topic that has not been taught.

A focused project using Python, pandas, JSON, or Flask can earn full credit when it meets the agreed scope and demonstrates meaningful learning. Choosing an advanced topic does not automatically earn more points.

1. Purpose and expectations

The main goal is your learning: applying what you know, investigating something you do not yet understand, and explaining your work clearly.

  • Choose a project connected to the course: AI, machine learning, or the data and application skills that support an AI workflow.
  • Identify your starting point and a specific learning goal. Your goal may involve deeper practice with a taught concept; a new algorithm is not required.
  • Complete a small, manageable scope and provide evidence of your work.
  • Explain how the important parts work together, check your results, and acknowledge limitations.

Important distinction: displaying or cleaning data with Flask or pandas is a foundation project, not an AI model. Foundation projects are accepted for this assignment; label your work accurately.

2. Midterm

Complete your selected midterm project, prepare your evidence and demonstration, and explain your learning.

Follow the Canvas assignment for the exact due date, submission settings, and presentation arrangements.

3. Choose your project path

Path A

Apply and build

Use taught skills to build a small working application or data workflow. Examples include a CSV viewer, cleaning tool, or regression project.

Show: input → processing → output, your code, and checks of expected and problematic cases.

Path B

Research and demonstrate

Investigate an AI concept and teach us how it works. Explain its parts, their interactions, a concrete example, and its limitations.

Show: an annotated walkthrough, small experiment, existing runnable example, or your own prototype. A full application is not required. Clearly credit existing demonstrations.

You may combine the paths, but doing both is not required. Whichever path you choose, a list of definitions or copied slides alone is not enough: use a concrete demonstration to make your understanding visible.

Using AI responsibly: focus on what you are learning

AI assistance is permitted for supporting work, but it must not replace your own understanding of the primary learning objective. Use AI strategically, verify its output, and be ready to explain your work.

Path A · Apply and build

AI can support non-focus components

If your learning goal is to use a trained machine learning model to generate and interpret predictions, you may ask AI to help build a supporting user interface—for example, a Flask page that accepts input and displays the prediction.

Your responsibility: understand the model workflow, what inputs it expects, how the prediction is produced and interpreted, and how you checked whether the result is reasonable. Be able to explain how the interface connects to the model, even if AI helped write that supporting code.

Path B · Research and demonstrate

AI can assist research and documentation

Rather than asking one AI model to choose a research project and produce a complete report for you, use AI to explore a topic you select. You may consult several AI models, compare their answers, investigate disagreements, and check important claims against credible sources. Agreement among AI models is not proof of accuracy.

Your responsibility: collect and evaluate evidence, conduct your own analysis, and develop your own conclusions. Afterward, you may use AI to organize, draft, or polish documentation or slides based on your verified findings and analysis. Review the final wording for accuracy and ensure it represents your actual work.

For both paths: identify the parts AI helped with, check generated code and claims, credit relevant sources, and explain the core concepts and decisions in your own words. AI is a tool for supporting learning—not a replacement for the learning being assessed.

Need a clear starting point?

Recommended foundation option: read a CSV file using Python, display its records on a Flask webpage, and add one useful feature such as a filter or summary. Check a normal file, an empty file, and a missing file.

Recommended model option: adapt the regression workflow taught in class to a new suitable dataset. Identify the target and inputs, split training and testing data, train the model, and report test MAE or RMSE. A Flask interface is optional unless it is part of your agreed scope.

Choose the option that offers meaningful practice for your current understanding, and confirm your scope with the instructor.

4. Project options — choose one or propose your own

Each description gives a possible small version. Optional extensions are not required. The research path can also use these topics as the basis for an explanation and demonstration.

01 · Foundation

Dataset Explorer

Build: load a CSV, show records on a Flask page, and provide one search or filter.

Explain/check: how rows reach the page; test matching and nonmatching searches. File upload is optional.

02 · Foundation

Data Cleaning Assistant

Build: identify missing values and duplicates, apply documented cleaning rules, and export a cleaned CSV.

Explain/check: show before/after counts and justify your rules. Do not remove data without explaining why.

03 · Foundation

Dataset Summary Dashboard

Build: show totals, averages, and two useful charts from a chosen dataset.

Explain/check: what each summary means; manually verify a small sample. A dashboard alone is not an AI model.

04 · Regression

Used-Car Price Predictor

Build: estimate car prices using suitable features such as age and mileage.

Explain/check: distinguish inputs from the price target, evaluate on held-out data, and discuss prediction errors.

05 · Regression

Bike Rental Demand Predictor

Build: predict rental count from suitable available features such as temperature and season.

Explain/check: compare predictions with actual counts and explain how categorical inputs are prepared if used.

06 · Regression

Delivery Time Predictor

Build: estimate delivery time from distance and other appropriate available inputs.

Explain/check: report test error in understandable time units and identify situations your data does not represent.

07 · Optional exploration

Regression Model Comparison

Demonstrate: compare Linear Regression with another regressor, such as a Decision Tree, on the same dataset.

Explain/check: use the same held-out split and metric for a fair comparison. Learning the second model is optional exploration.

08 · Optional exploration

Review Sentiment Classifier

Demonstrate: classify a short review as positive or negative using a suitable labeled dataset.

Explain/check: how text becomes model input, classification versus regression, and examples the model gets wrong.

09 · Optional exploration

Similar-Item Recommender

Demonstrate: suggest similar movies or books from their descriptions using text similarity.

Explain/check: why an item was suggested. Similarity recommendations do not necessarily learn a user's personal preferences.

10 · Foundation or exploration

Course FAQ Assistant

Demonstrate: retrieve an answer from a small course FAQ collection and show an unavailable-information message when no answer matches.

Explain/check: test known and unknown questions. Keyword search is a foundation option; semantic search is an optional extension.

11 · Optional exploration

Spam Classification

Demonstrate: label sample messages as spam or not spam with a suitable dataset.

Explain/check: show correct and incorrect predictions and explain the difference between falsely flagging a normal message and missing spam.

12 · Optional exploration

Clustering Similar Records

Demonstrate: group records using two numerical features and visualize the groups.

Explain/check: explain unlabeled data, feature scaling, and the basis for similarity. Do not assume each cluster has a proven real-world meaning.

13 · Regression exploration

Overfitting Investigation

Demonstrate: compare a simple and a more complex model using training and test performance.

Explain/check: show whether your results suggest overfitting. Report what actually happened; the expected pattern is not guaranteed.

14 · Data investigation

How Data Quality Affects a Model

Demonstrate: compare a documented baseline workflow with an improved cleaning workflow using the same model and data split.

Explain/check: both workflows must handle data sufficiently to run. Fit learned preprocessing on training data only. Improvement is not required; explain the observed result.

15 · Optional research and demonstration

How Agentic AI Works

Demonstrate: trace a small goal-driven example, such as answering a sales question with a CSV-reading tool.

Explain/check: goal → action/tool selection → tool result → next action or final answer. Identify the model, instructions, tools, state, and stopping rule. Show a missing-file or unavailable-column case.

A chatbot alone does not establish agentic behavior; show how actions are selected and results guide the next step. An existing framework or local mock tool is acceptable if clearly identified. Paid services and autonomous real-world actions are not required.

Choose another course-related application or AI concept. Describe what you will learn and what a small completed result will look like.

5. Confirm your scope before building

Share a short proposal with the instructor using these prompts. Scope confirmation is intended to give you clear direction and a realistic target.

  1. Project title and path: What will you build or investigate?
  2. Starting point: What can you already do?
  3. Learning goal: What will you practice, understand more deeply, or learn?
  4. Minimum result: List two or three specific outcomes you will demonstrate.
  5. Tools and data: What do you need, and where will it come from?
  6. Verification: How will you check your result or explanation?

If the project becomes too large or you encounter a major blocker, discuss a smaller scope with the instructor rather than silently changing your goals.

6. What to submit

  1. Your project materials: source code and necessary sample data for a build, or your research explanation/slides and demonstration materials for a research project. Credit data and external code. Provide a public source or sample when the original data cannot be shared.
  2. A short README: project purpose, agreed scope, learning goal, file descriptions, and numbered instructions to run or reproduce the demonstration. List required packages and setup steps where applicable.
  3. Demonstration evidence: screenshots, recorded walkthrough, or reproducible outputs showing the important steps and results. Be prepared to explain the demonstration in the presentation format announced in Canvas.
  4. Verification record: a small table showing case/input, expected behavior, observed result, and pass/fail or explanation. Include a normal case and at least one edge or failure case. For research, check source claims and demonstrate a limitation or counterexample.
  5. Learning reflection and sources: explain your starting point, what you learned, one challenge and how you addressed it, and remaining limitations. Cite sources and disclose borrowed code, frameworks, and AI assistance.

Reflection template

Use these prompts to write a short reflection. One or two specific sentences per prompt are enough. Refer to examples from your own project; a long essay is not required.

  1. My starting point: Before this project, I already knew how to ______. I needed more practice with / wanted to learn ______.
  2. What I learned: I learned or understood ______ more clearly. An example from my project that shows this is ______.
  3. A challenge I faced: I had difficulty with ______. I investigated it by ______.
  4. How I responded: I tried ______. The result was ______. If the issue is unresolved, what I understand so far is ______.
  5. Current limitations: My project or demonstration currently cannot / does not address ______.
  6. My next step: If I continued this work, I would ______ because ______.

For a research project, your challenge may be understanding a concept, comparing sources, or explaining a demonstration. Describe what actually happened; you do not need to invent a coding error or claim that every issue was solved.

Submit through the Canvas assignment. Use a clearly named folder or ZIP when uploading multiple files. If submitting a repository link, verify that the instructor can access it.

Not automatically required: a full end-to-end product, Flask interface, trained model, new algorithm, public deployment, paid API, or automated test suite. Include these only when they are part of your agreed project scope.

7. Demonstrate understanding and check your work

  • Application: trace a sample input through the code to the output. Try an empty, missing, invalid, or out-of-range input where relevant.
  • Machine learning: distinguish training from testing, use an appropriate metric, and discuss mistakes. Do not evaluate only on training data or include the answer in the input features.
  • Research: explain concepts in your own words, use credible sources, trace a concrete example, and distinguish verified behavior from claims or assumptions.
  • Use of assistance: tools and AI assistance may support your work, but you must understand the submitted result, verify it, and identify what you created or modified.

Use public or appropriately shareable data. Keep passwords and API keys out of your submission. Synthetic data is acceptable when clearly labeled and its limitations are explained.

8. Grading rubric — 100 points

The same four criteria apply to both project paths. Assessment considers your agreed scope and the quality of your evidence, not how advanced the topic sounds.
